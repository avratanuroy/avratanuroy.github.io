Research

Combinatorial trajectory theory

I have formulated the enumeration of feasible road-user trajectories as a system of linear time-invariant difference equations and obtained closed-form analytical solutions using the unilateral Z-transform, the inverse Z-transform and matrix methods. The symbolic solutions were derived in SageMath and MATLAB and verified against numerical simulation in Python. This work was presented as a poster at Traffic and Granular Flow 2024, Lyon, France.

Lane-free representation of the conflict field

I have generalised this theory from lane-based roads to lane-free space through a density-adaptive Voronoi tessellation and spectral graph analysis. I established a formal normalization identity linking the combinatorial baseline to a Bayesian Dirichlet–Multinomial Markov model under the maximum-entropy principle, and built a dynamic conflict-precursor detector on this representation.

Game theory of right-of-way negotiation

I have formulated pairwise road-user encounters as non-cooperative games whose payoffs derive from a parameter-free, kinematics-based danger index. I derived the mixed-strategy Nash equilibria analytically, established their invariance to behavioural parameters, and quantified the efficiency loss of uncoordinated interaction through the price of anarchy. A central finding is that this loss does not follow conflict frequency and is greatest between fast and slow road-user classes, such as cars and pedestrians.

Equilibrium-based intervention design

I have converted the price of anarchy into a design criterion. I derived an analytical threshold for the strength of a priority rule required to eliminate the inefficient equilibrium, and developed a closed-loop, multi-objective framework that ranks safety interventions by safety, throughput and residual inefficiency.

Research beyond the Ph.D. · Mean field games

Ongoing, independent of the doctoral thesis. I have formulated a heterogeneous-action-space mean field game in which each road-user class has its own action space and the classes are coupled through an asymmetric cross-class interaction matrix. I derived the coupled Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman and Fokker–Planck system and a Hopf–Cole reduction to a fixed point over positive eigenfunctions, extending the theory beyond its standard monotone setting. The finite-element equilibrium solver is implemented in Python and calibrated on drone trajectory data.

Software

I have implemented the complete analytical pipeline of my doctoral research in Python, covering Voronoi construction, Bayesian estimation, equilibrium computation, calibration, bootstrap uncertainty quantification, a dynamic conflict-precursor detector, and closed-loop multi-objective evaluation and selection of design interventions. The code, organised by thesis chapter and documented for reproduction, is available at github.com/avratanuroy/traffic-conflict-game-theory.
